MICROCONTROLLERS · AUDIO SYNTHESIS

Programmable Birdsong Synthesizer

Built a real-time embedded audio synthesizer on the RP2350 that generates, records, accelerates, and sequences user-controlled frequency patterns to reproduce rapidly modulated birdsong.

C RP2350 DDS SPI ADC Interrupts Embedded Audio

01

System Overview

The synthesizer allows a user to generate and manipulate audio in real time using a slide potentiometer and matrix keypad. Moving the potentiometer continuously adjusts the generated frequency over a range of approximately 0-10 kHz.

Audio samples are generated digitally on the RP2350 and sent over SPI to an external DAC, where the digital samples are converted into an analog signal for playback through an audio output.

Beyond generating a simple tone, the system can record frequency trajectories, assign individual recordings to keypad buttons, accelerate their playback to create rapid bird-like frequency modulation, and combine recordings into programmable sequences.

02

Real-Time Audio Synthesis

Audio is generated using direct digital synthesis (DDS). A hardware timer invokes the synthesis interrupt service routine every 20 μs, producing a 50 kHz sample rate.

During each interrupt, a phase accumulator is advanced according to the desired output frequency. The upper bits of the accumulator index a 256-entry sine lookup table, producing the next waveform sample.

The resulting sample is packaged into the DAC command format and transmitted over SPI. Repeating this process at a fixed sampling rate produces the continuous analog tone heard at the output.

03

Real-Time Performance Verification

Generating audio at 50 kHz gives the processor only 20 μs between consecutive samples, so the synthesis ISR must finish comfortably within that timing budget.

To measure execution time on the physical system, I asserted a GPIO pin immediately upon entering the synthesis ISR and cleared it before returning. Measuring the resulting pulse width on an oscilloscope directly captures the time spent executing the ISR.

The measured execution time was approximately 2.16 μs, corresponding to about 10.8% of the available 20 μs sampling period. This leaves approximately 17.84 μs before the next audio sample must be generated.

04

Analog Pitch Control

A slide potentiometer provides continuous control over the synthesizer's pitch. The RP2350 ADC samples the potentiometer voltage and maps its digital value to an output frequency ranging from approximately 0 to 10 kHz.

Rather than directly changing the waveform itself, the measured frequency determines the DDS phase increment. Larger phase increments move through the sine lookup table more quickly, producing a higher output frequency.

Potentiometer → ADC value → Desired frequency → DDS phase increment

05

Keypad Input & Debouncing

A matrix keypad provides the primary digital interface for controlling recording, playback, composition, and tone output.

Mechanical switches do not transition cleanly between logic levels when pressed or released. Without debouncing, a single physical keypress can therefore appear as several rapid input events.

I used a four-state finite-state machine - NOT_PRESSED, MAYBE_PRESSED, PRESSED, and MAYBE_NOT_PRESSED - to verify that a key remains stable before accepting the transition.

09

Challenges & Debugging

Many of the most difficult bugs appeared only after the audio, user-input, and control subsystems were integrated.

01

When Debugging Changed the Bug

While debugging the synthesis interrupt, it was tempting to add print statements inside the ISR to report state changes and intermediate values. However, UART output is extremely slow relative to the audio sampling deadline.

At 115200 baud with a typical 8-N-1 UART frame, transmitting even a single ASCII character takes approximately 86.8 μs. The audio synthesis ISR, however, must execute once every 20 μs to maintain the 50 kHz sample rate. A blocking character transmission inside the ISR could therefore consume more than four complete sample periods.

This created a particularly misleading debugging problem: instrumentation intended to expose timing-related behavior could itself cause missed deadlines and change how the original bug appeared.

Solution: I moved time-sensitive debugging away from formatted serial output and used GPIO instrumentation instead. By setting a GPIO on ISR entry and clearing it on exit, I could observe execution timing externally with an oscilloscope without significantly disturbing the real-time behavior of the system.
02

Breadboard Parasitics & Keypad Reliability

The keypad interface relied on pull up/down resistances to distinguish rows and columns.

Parasitic capacitance from the breadboard and jumper wires, combined with pull-up/pull-down resistance and switch contact behavior, created finite RC settling times at GPIO inputs. Reading a keypad line too quickly after changing the driven row would therefore sometimes sample the signal before it has fully settled.

Mechanical switch bounce added another source of transient input behavior, making a single physical press capable of producing multiple apparent transitions.

Solution: To address this, the firmware had to account for the keypad's real-world electrical characteristics rather than treating inputs as ideal digital signals. A stable scan timing combined with the four-state debounce FSM ensured that a transition had to persist before the software accepted it as a valid press or release.
03

Preventing Operating Modes from Interacting

As functionality expanded, the synthesizer developed several distinct operating behaviors: live tone generation, recording, individual playback, composition entry, and composed-sequence playback.

Early implementations made it possible for state associated with one behavior to remain active after entering another. For example, playback state, recording state, or a previous key event could unintentionally affect the newly selected mode. These interactions produced behavior that appeared unpredictable because each feature worked correctly in isolation.

Solution: I separated the operating behaviors into explicit modes and made mode transitions responsible for initializing or clearing the relevant state. This prevented stale recording or playback state from leaking into another mode and made the overall control flow substantially easier to reason about.
04

Verifying the Real-Time Timing Budget

With a 50 kHz sampling rate, every audio sample must be generated within a 20 μs window. As more functionality was added to the system, it became important to verify that the synthesis ISR still met this deadline rather than assuming that it did.

I instrumented the ISR by asserting a GPIO immediately upon entry and clearing it immediately before exit. The pulse width could then be measured directly with an oscilloscope.

Result: The ISR measured approximately 2.16 μs, or about 10.8% of the available 20 μs sample period, leaving approximately 17.84 μs of timing margin.
